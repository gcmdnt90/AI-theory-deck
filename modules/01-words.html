Module 1

How the machine makes words

A 1948 idea, running at industrial scale.

The module's claim: the idea behind language models is from 1948. What changed since is scale, data and architecture.

By the end of the module the machine should have moved, in the listener's head, from oracle to sampler.

1948 — language as a game of probability

  • Shannon: text is a source that emits symbols with a probability.
  • Sampling from those statistics gives sentences that sound English and say nothing.
  • 1951: he measures how predictable English is — 0.6–1.3 bits per letter.
  • An LLM estimates the same distribution: learned, not counted by hand.
THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD FOR THE LETTERS THAT THE TIME OF WHO EVER TOLD THE PROBLEM FOR AN UNEXPECTED. Text generated by Shannon in 1948 by sampling word-pair statistics.
Fluent and empty: the machine was already doing that in 1948.

The 1948 sample is worth reading aloud: it sounds like English and says nothing. It is the literal ancestor of hallucination, and module 5 comes back to it.

A language model is an enormous learned estimator of that same distribution. Everything else — memory, tools, rules — is engineering built around this single operation.

Going deeper. Shannon (1948), A Mathematical Theory of Communication, for the approximations to English; Shannon (1951), Prediction and Entropy of Printed English, for the measured 0.6–1.3 bits per letter with a hundred letters of context. It is the one number in the module worth saying out loud. The formula is deliberately kept off the slide: the widget two slides later does that job.

From rules to learned patterns

Three jumps: from rules to data, from data to vectors, from completion to instruction.

Two minutes, no more: the timeline is a list of technical choices, not a story. The three stops that matter:

2013 (Mikolov) — words become vectors, and closeness in that space is closeness in meaning. It is the same mechanism that returns in module 3 under the name retrieval.
2017 (Vaswani) — attention compares every word with every other in parallel, and that is what makes large-scale training practical. The encoder-decoder does not get opened.
2022 (Ouyang, InstructGPT) — training with human feedback adds following instructions to merely completing text. It is the difference between a model that continues a sentence and the assistant everyone uses daily, and it is why module 4 works at all.

Sources. Mikolov (2013); Vaswani (2017); Brown (2020); Ouyang (2022).

Tokens, not words

  • The machine never sees letters: it sees sub-word pieces, chosen by frequency in the data.
  • Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ 3/4 of a word (in English).
  • The token is the unit of price and of memory — and languages do not cost the same.
Hover or tap a token to see how frequent it is.

Real tokenizer: cl100k_base, the GPT-4 family encoding. Piece colour shows how frequent it is.

A live, low-load slide. Type one of the room's own words into the box — a trade term, a client name: the rarer it is, the more pieces it breaks into, and the colour shows it.

The "letters" preset is the fastest myth-killer in the deck: " strawberry" is ONE token, so the model never saw the individual r's. It does not fail out of stupidity: it fails because the letters never reach it.

The Italian/English comparison is the moment an owner cares about. The same sentence costs 14 tokens in Italian and 6 in English (3.4 against 7.0 characters per token): same meaning, more than twice the cost and twice the window consumed.

Going deeper. Sennrich et al. (2016) for BPE; OpenAI for the rule of thumb of 1 token ≈ 4 characters; Petrov et al. (NeurIPS 2023) for the gap between languages.

One operation, repeated

  • It works out the odds of the next piece, picks one, adds it, starts again.
  • What it writes becomes part of what it reads: early choices steer every later one.
  • It consults no archive — it chooses, one piece at a time.

The point to hammer is the loop: every word written enters what gets read at the next step, so the early choices steer all the later ones. Hallucination, creativity and fluency all come out of this same mechanism.

The bars are real probabilities, measured once against the OpenAI API (gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct, temperature 1). They are not normalised: the grey bar is the rest of the vocabulary.

The comparison that does the work: "the capital of Italy is" gives Rome at 81%, "the coin was struck in" gives its top candidate at 8%. Same machine, opposite confidence — and it writes in the same tone either way. Worth noticing too that some candidates (pre, arg, es) are word pieces, not words: that is the tokenizer from two slides ago.

Temperature: the randomness dial

Low · 0.2–0.5

The distribution tightens onto the most likely candidate.

→ extraction, classification, repeatable output

High · 1.0–2.0

The distribution flattens: rare candidates get in.

→ variants, rewrites, brainstorming

Always

It redistributes probability, it adds no knowledge. At T = 0 a wrong answer just becomes a repeatable wrong answer.

T = 0.7

Real logits: the log of the measured probabilities. The recalculation with T is the actual formula.

Randomness is a setting, not a personality.

The best interactive moment in the deck: drag the slider slowly to both extremes and let the bars do the talking. The base is the five real candidates from the previous slide, renormalised against each other, and the slider applies the real formula: softmax(logit/T).

If only one minute is left, the line to say is the third card: temperature moves probability, not knowledge. At T = 0 the model repeats the error instead of correcting it, and that is the misconception module 5 takes apart again.
