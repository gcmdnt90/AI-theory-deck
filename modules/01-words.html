Module 1

How the machine makes words

One old idea, running very fast.

Thesis: the idea behind LLMs is from 1948. What changed is scale, data and architecture. By the end of this module they should see the machine as a sampler, not an oracle.

1948 — language as a game of probability

Claude Shannon described text as a source that emits words with a certain probability.

Guessing the next word well means knowing the language well.

Everything today still sits on that bet.
Sources: Shannon (1948) A Mathematical Theory of Communication; Shannon (1951) Prediction and Entropy of Printed English. An LLM is a very large learned estimator of Shannon's next-symbol distribution; everything else is engineering around that bet. Do not put the formula on screen - the widget two slides from now does the work.

From rules to learned patterns

We stopped telling the machine the rules and let it infer them from data.
Two minutes, no more. The one architectural fact worth saying out loud: in 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al.) let every word be compared with every other word in parallel, which made training at scale practical. Do not open the encoder-decoder. Scaling era in one breath: 2018-today, bigger models, then human feedback turned them into assistants. Sources: Mikolov (2013); Vaswani (2017); Brown (2020); Ouyang (2022).

Three levels, one machine

macro Judgement

The human who decides whether the output is acceptable.

meso The system

Memory, tools, skills, connectors. This is where you work.

micro The base

Probabilities over the next word. Always on.

Reading the machine at the wrong level is the most common mistake.
The deck's spine - the one slide to defend if time runs out. Cristianini's mesoscopic level: micro = neurons, meso = the abstract ideas the model built by itself, macro = behaviour read almost psychologically. Guard the flank: this is a claim about the right LEVEL of description (Marr 1982), NOT that the model became a mind. "It's just predicting words" is true at micro and useless at meso, which is where their actual work happens. Every later module answers: which level are we on?

Tokens, not words

The machine reads sub-word pieces. That is why some "obvious" tasks are mechanically awkward for it.

Hover or tap a token to see why it was split.
Live and low-load. Type one of their own words into the box - a coin term, a client name. The "count the r in strawberry" preset is the fastest myth-buster in the deck: it fails not because the model is stupid but because it never sees letters. Source: Sennrich et al. (2016).

One operation, repeated

The machine works out the odds of the next piece of text, picks one, adds it, and starts again.

Fluency is a loop, not a lookup.
