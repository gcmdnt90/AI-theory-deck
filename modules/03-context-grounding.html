Where it gets what it does not know

How the machine gets to know a business.

The module with the most direct commercial value. The central distinction: context is what the model has in front of it right now, memory is what survives between one session and the next. Both sit "around".

The context window

Context is the model's desk: everything it can see while answering this one question.

Every question re-sends the whole conversation. When it no longer fits, the oldest part is summarised or cut — and it is worth paying attention to when that happens.

The desk is wiped after every question unless things are put back on it.

The image of the wiped desk is the one to take away. On its own it explains why a new chat "forgets", why long threads get worse, and why the instruction file of module 4 matters.

The context problem

Declared windows went from ~2,000 tokens (2020) to ~1,000,000 on some models: from a few pages to a few hundred.

But the declared window is not the usable one:

  • what sits at the start and at the end is used better than what ends up buried in the middle;
  • at 32,000 tokens 11 models out of 13 fall below half the score they reach on short texts.
  • and when the window fills up, the oldest part is summarised or cut - automatically, lossily, and with no warning.

→ a few relevant documents beat “paste everything in”

What goes in, and in what order, still matters.

Chart: Liu et al., “Lost in the Middle” (TACL 2024), gpt-3.5-turbo over 20 retrieved documents. The 32,000-token figure: NoLiMa (ICML 2025).

The "we'll just paste everything in" reflex is worth heading off before it forms.

The third bullet is the ready answer to the question that always comes: the three-hour conversation where the chatbot "forgets" the beginning. It is not a fault and it is not a privacy choice: past a certain number of tokens the old part is compacted or cut, and granularity is lost. Every model has this limit; the size changes, the mechanism does not. Never say the system "learns" or "remembers": it puts text back on the desk, and that is all.

The numbers, if asked: GPT-3 sat at ~2,000 tokens, frontier models declare up to ~1,000,000, which is roughly 700-750 thousand words (a token is about three quarters of a word: not a million and a half).

The two lines on the slide say two different things and should stay distinct. The first is position: the chart is from 2023 on gpt-3.5-turbo, so it is not the last word. The second is recent and harder: in the NoLiMa benchmark (ICML 2025), at 32,000 tokens 11 models out of 13 drop below half their short-text score, and GPT-4o goes from 99.3% to 69.7%. It is the ready answer to "but the windows are enormous now": enormous yes, usable in full no.

The closing arrow is the bridge to retrieval: if a few relevant documents beat the pile, something has to choose them.

Retrieval: find first, then write

Once: it is indexed

Documents are cut into pieces; each piece becomes a position on a map of meanings. They stay outside the model.

Each question: it is searched

The question becomes a position too. Only the nearest pieces come in: that is the anchoring, the grounding.

When it fails

If the question is worded far from the document's wording, the right pieces never come in. And an answer arrives anyway.

question → into numbers → search by meaning → most similar pieces → LLM → answer

The company's documents are not trained into the model: they are handed to it, one question at a time.

The most useful diagram this audience will actually adopt. The video carries the point on its own: the wall of documents does not move, the empty slots stay empty, only a few pieces travel.

The first card is the step missing from every explanation heard elsewhere, and it is what takes apart the three commonest wrong ideas about "uploading the documents": it is not a Ctrl+F over the archive, it is not the model learning the document, it is not indexing by name and date. It is split, converted into numbers, and the index is saved: once, and outside the model. The map of meanings is the same one seen in module 1 at the "vectors" stop.

The second card is where the word gets named: anchoring the answer to a retrieved source is grounding, and it is the title of the demo that follows.

The third card is worth saying aloud, with the example: "how do I handle holidays?" finds nothing, "how do I request annual leave?" finds it at once. Same question, different words. Search works by closeness of meaning, not exact words; if the question lands far from how the document is written, the right passage does not come back — and nothing warns you. That is why the demo shows the retrieved passages instead of trusting the answer.

The two operational consequences to land: nothing is memorised, so updating a document changes the answers immediately with no retraining; and the model can only answer with what retrieval found, so the quality of retrieval is the quality of the answer.
