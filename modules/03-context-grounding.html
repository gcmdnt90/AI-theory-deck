Context, memory, grounding

How the machine gets to know a business.

The module with the highest direct business value. Core distinction: context is what is in front of the model right now; memory is what survives between sessions. Both are "around".

The context problem

Declared windows went from ~2,000 tokens (2020) to ~1,000,000 on some models: from a few pages to a few hundred.

But the declared window is not the usable one:

  • what sits at the start and at the end is used better than what ends up buried in the middle;
  • at 32,000 tokens 11 models out of 13 fall below half the score they reach on short texts.

→ a few relevant documents beat “paste everything in”

What goes in, and in what order, still matters.

Chart: Liu et al., “Lost in the Middle” (TACL 2024), gpt-3.5-turbo over 20 retrieved documents. The 32,000-token figure: NoLiMa (ICML 2025).

Head off the "we'll just paste everything in" reflex before it forms. The numbers, if asked: GPT-3 sat at ~2,000 tokens, frontier models declare up to ~1,000,000, which is roughly 700-750 thousand words (a token is about three quarters of a word - NOT one and a half million words). The two lines say different things and should stay distinct. The first is about position: the chart is from 2023 on gpt-3.5-turbo, so do not sell it as the last word. The second is recent and harder: in the NoLiMa benchmark (ICML 2025), at 32,000 tokens 11 of 13 models drop below half their short-text score, and GPT-4o goes from 99.3% to 69.7%. That is the ready answer to "but windows are huge now": huge yes, usable in full no. Three ways not to start from scratch

When the desk fills up, something has to go. Three mechanisms decide what stays.

1 · Summarisation

The oldest part is compressed into notes. Automatic and lossy: details disappear without warning.

→ “why has it forgotten what it was told an hour ago?”

2 · Persistent notes

Facts written to a file, put back on the desk at the start of every session. Explicit and correctable — but someone has to write them.

→ module 4

3 · Searchable archive

The documents stay outside. Each question triggers a search, and only the relevant pieces are inserted.

→ next slide

None of the three writes inside the model: all three rewrite the desk.
Open on the video, which shows the first mechanism end to end: the container fills, hits saturation ("context saturation reached"), and the contents collapse into a summary bar. Card 1 is the ready answer to the question that always comes: the three-hour conversation in which the chatbot "forgets" the beginning. It is not a fault and not a privacy choice: past a certain number of tokens the old part is compacted or cut, and granularity is lost. Every model has this limit; the size changes, the mechanism does not. Honest caveat: this is documented product behaviour of the chat interfaces, not a benchmark result - the two numbered lines are on the previous slide. Card 2 is the bridge to module 4: the memory that can be inspected and corrected is the one someone wrote. Card 3 is the bridge to the next slide: if the documents stay outside, something has to choose which pieces come in. The takeaway carries the rest of the module: none of the three touches the parameters. Immediate operational consequence: update a document and the answers change at once, with no retraining. Never say the system "learns" or "remembers". It puts text back on the desk.

Retrieval: find first, then write

Once: it is indexed

Documents are cut into pieces; each piece becomes a position on a map of meanings. They stay outside the model.

Each question: it is searched

The question becomes a position too. Only the nearest pieces come in: that is the anchoring, the grounding.

When it fails

If the question is worded far from the document's wording, the right pieces never come in. And an answer arrives anyway.

question → into numbers → search by meaning → most similar pieces → LLM → answer

The company's documents are not trained into the model: they are handed to it, one question at a time.
The most useful pattern this audience will actually adopt. The video carries the point on its own: the wall of documents does not move, the empty slots stay visible, only a few pieces travel. Card 1 is the step missing from most explanations, and it dismantles the three most common wrong ideas about "uploading documents": it is not a Ctrl+F over the archive, it is not the model learning the document, it is not indexing by name and date. Cut, converted into numbers, saved as an index - once, and outside the model. The map of meanings is the one seen in module 1 at the "vectors" stop. Card 2: name the word here. Anchoring an answer to a retrieved source is grounding, and it is the title of the demo that follows. Card 3 is the part to say out loud, with the example: "how do I handle holidays?" finds nothing, "how do I request annual leave?" finds it at once. Same question, different words. Search works by proximity of meaning, not by exact words; if the question falls far from the way the document is written, the right passage does not come back - and nothing warns you. That is why the demo shows the retrieved passages instead of trusting the answer. The two operational consequences to land: (1) nothing is memorised, so updating a document changes the answers at once, with no retraining; (2) the model can only answer from what retrieval found, so retrieval quality IS answer quality. Sources: Lewis et al. (2020) RAG; Karpukhin et al. (2020) Dense Passage Retrieval; Mikolov et al. (2013).
