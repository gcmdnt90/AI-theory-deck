MCP — reaching the company's systems

"Think of MCP like a USB-C port for AI applications. Just as USB-C provides a standardized way to connect electronic devices, MCP provides a standardized way to connect AI applications to external systems." — official MCP documentation

DiscoverThe connector says what it can do.
CallThe agent sends a structured request.
ReturnThe result comes back as new context.

Without a standard, n applications and m tools need n×m pieces of custom wiring. With one protocol, n+m are enough — and the systems the company already has do not have to be rebuilt to be reachable. It moves requests and results: it does not pool the data.

Same questions as any integration: whose credentials, what scope, how much trust.

Live window, about two minutes: the connector list open in two different products, nothing more. It is there to show this is not one vendor's arrangement. The quotation is literal and comes from the protocol's own documentation. Deliver it as a quotation, not as a house metaphor. The n×m to n+m arithmetic is the mechanism; the USB-C port is its illustration. If someone wants a number: five applications and six tools make thirty custom integrations against eleven. Timeline, if the question is "what if they change their minds?": open protocol published on 25 November 2024, adopted by OpenAI in March 2025 and by Google in April 2025, and donated on 9 December 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund of the Linux Foundation co-founded with OpenAI and Block. It is no longer governed by one company. Do not go near the technical specification. The line that matters commercially is the one in the card: their existing systems do not have to be rebuilt to be reachable. The three moves are the mechanism. Spec, verification, and limits outside the model

1. The spec stays with whoever commissions the work

Every degree of freedom left open is closed with a plausible default, drawn from the training distribution rather than from the case at hand. Specifying means listing what is not left to the tool to decide, starting from which decision the work must enable.

2. Verification runs against the source

The criterion is fixed before generation, the check runs after, against the source document and not against the output. A criterion written after reading the answer bends to the answer.

The three tiers are chosen by reversibility and cost of error, not by perceived seriousness, and applied at the tool, not in the prompt.

Always doReversible, low cost: runs on its own.
Ask firstReversible but expensive: confirmation required before acting.
Never doIrreversible: blocked at the tool, out of the model's reach.
This slide introduces nothing: it names three things the demo has just made happen. Point 1 is the sixth beat (criteria stated aloud before the run), point 2 is the eighth (the table checked against the PDFs), point 3 is the fourth (the house rules overridden). If the demo landed, this is pointing, not teaching. The line above the tiers used to be a fourth card: leave it unspoken, it reads on its own. The criterion for the three tiers is reversibility and cost, not perceived seriousness: sending the wrong email is irreversible and belongs in the third row even though it feels trivial, while rewriting a draft is reversible and belongs in the first even though the text matters. Run the exercise out loud on a task of theirs. Karpathy's automation thesis: traditional software automates what can be specified; LLMs automate what can be VERIFIED, so it pays to make one's own work verifiable. Cherny, creator of Claude Code: "Give Claude a way to verify its work... it will 2-3x the quality" - present it as a practitioner's estimate, not as a measurement. Shankar et al. (UIST 2024) on criteria drift: grading real outputs changes the criteria, so spec and verification feed each other; it is one loop, not a pipeline. The story to tell, and it is the best content on this slide: Karpathy's agent matched payments to accounts by email address instead of a stable identifier. Plausible code, wrong system design, caught only by human judgement - and no line-by-line code review would have found it, because every line was correct.
