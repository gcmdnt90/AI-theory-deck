The difference between a chatbot and an agent is the scaffolding around the same model, not a bigger model.

The module's hinge, and the only place an agentic system gets defined. It used to be defined in module 2 as well, two modules earlier and in the abstract: one of the three places the same idea was being repeated. Here the definition arrives where it is about to be used — the next slide is how tools reach their systems, and the demo after that runs the loop on their own folder.

The loop is worth saying aloud — decides, acts, observes — because those are exactly the three words that will narrate the Cowork demo, and they have to be recognisable when they come back.

Up to here a text has been written; from here that text commands something that acts. The difference in one line, if asked: a chatbot answers turn by turn, an agentic system chases a goal longer than one turn.

On cost, which someone always raises: the answer is on the first card. The loop decides for itself when to stop, so a badly set-up agent can repeat useless rounds — which is exactly why the module's last slide writes the spec first and verifies after.

MCP — reaching the company's systems

"Think of MCP like a USB-C port for AI applications. Just as USB-C provides a standardized way to connect electronic devices, MCP provides a standardized way to connect AI applications to external systems." — official MCP documentation

DiscoverThe connector says what it can do.
CallThe agent sends a structured request.
ReturnThe result comes back as new context.

Without a standard, n applications and m tools need n×m pieces of custom wiring. With one protocol, n+m are enough — and the systems the company already has do not have to be rebuilt to be reachable. It moves requests and results: it does not pool the data.

Same questions as any integration: whose credentials, what scope, how much trust.

A live window, about two minutes: the connector list open in two different products, nothing more. It exists to show this is not one vendor's thing.

The quotation is literal and comes from the protocol's own documentation: "Think of MCP like a USB-C port for AI applications." It should be delivered as a quotation, not as our own line.

The n×m → n+m arithmetic is the mechanism, the USB-C port is its illustration: five applications and six tools make thirty bespoke integrations against eleven.

Timeline, for "what if they change their minds?": open protocol published 25 November 2024, adopted by OpenAI in March 2025 and by Google in April 2025, donated on 9 December 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund of the Linux Foundation co-founded with OpenAI and Block. It is no longer governed by a single company.

The technical specification is not worth going near. The sentence that matters commercially is the one on the card: their existing systems do not have to be rebuilt to become reachable.

The three moves are the mechanism. The third ties the module back to module 3: a tool's result does not "enter the model", it comes back onto the desk as new context. This is where the risk seed gets planted, because every connector is also an attack surface: module 5 opens by exploiting exactly this.

Spec, verification, and limits outside the model

1. The spec stays with whoever commissions the work

Every degree of freedom left open is closed with a plausible default, drawn from the training distribution rather than from the case at hand. Specifying means listing what is not left to the tool to decide, starting from which decision the work must enable.

2. Verification runs against the source

The criterion is fixed before generation, the check runs after, against the source document and not against the output. A criterion written after reading the answer bends to the answer.

The three tiers are chosen by reversibility and cost of error, not by perceived seriousness, and applied at the tool, not in the prompt.

Always doReversible, low cost: runs on its own.
Ask firstReversible but expensive: confirmation required before acting.
Never doIrreversible: blocked at the tool, out of the model's reach.

This slide introduces nothing: it names three things the demo has just made happen. Point 1 is beat six (the criteria stated aloud before launching), point 2 is beat eight (the table checked against the source documents), point 3 is beat four (the house rules overridden). If the demo landed, pointing is enough here.

The line above the three tiers used to be a fourth card: it reads on its own and does not need saying.

The criterion for the three tiers is reversibility and cost of error, not perceived seriousness: sending a wrong email is irreversible and belongs in the third row even if it looks trivial, while rewriting a draft is reversible and belongs in the first even if the text matters. The exercise is done aloud, with one of their own tasks.

Going deeper. Karpathy's thesis on automation: traditional software automates what you can specify, LLMs automate what you can verify, so it pays to make your own work verifiable. Cherny, creator of Claude Code: "give Claude a way to verify its own work and you double or triple the quality" — a practitioner's estimate, not a measurement. Shankar et al. (UIST 2024) on criteria drift: evaluating real output changes the criteria, so spec and verification feed each other; it is a loop, not an assembly line.

The anecdote is the best content on the slide: Karpathy's agent matched payments to accounts by email address instead of a stable identifier. Plausible code, wrong design, caught only by human judgement — and no line-by-line re-reading would have found it, because every line was correct.
