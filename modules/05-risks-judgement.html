Module 5

Risks and judgement

What goes wrong and who stays responsible.

The last module, and the one that makes everything else defensible: the previous four showed what the machine can be made to do, this one says on what conditions the result holds up in front of a client.

Three owners of risk — the model, the person using it, the data — and then who decides. That structure has to survive someone forgetting every example, and it is the one thing worth saying twice.

It is not a module of warnings, and saying so up front matters: every risk named here already has its counter-move in the earlier modules — ground it in the documents (M3), write the rules and verify (M4), keep destructive actions out of the tool's reach. Without that framing the room stiffens and the closing demo reads as a punishment.

It ends on the commitment question: one thing each person will try in the next 30 days, written down and not just spoken.

model What the model gets wrong

Hallucination

Fluent, confident, false. Structural, not a bug: it is reduced by anchoring it to documents and checking it. Not removed.

Bias

With no references of its own it returns the average of what has already been written: the words, patterns and structures the web is full of.

Agreeableness

Push back and it retracts — even when it was right. Finetuning rewards the answers people approve of.

The first two come from the engine of Module 1 — plausible text, not true text. The third comes from how it was finetuned: on human judgements.

The causal thread starts in module 1: the engine samples from a distribution, with no oracle of truth attached. The first two cards come from there.

Hallucination. It is also the correct answer to one of the test items: it is structural, it is reduced by grounding in documents and human verification, and it is not eliminated. Worth saying in those words.

Bias, the measured version. Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances 10(28), eadn5290 (2024) — online, peer-reviewed experiment. With a single AI-generated idea, stories become more original to their writers (+8.1% novelty) but 10.7% more similar to each other. This is not a moral judgement about training data: it is the average of what has already been written resurfacing whenever a reference of one's own is missing. It is why module 3's ladder changes the result, and why in the demo the disciplined prompt pulls a different structure out of the same material. Where the model touches people — hiring, assessment, credit — bias also becomes a legal matter (AI Act, high-risk systems): name it, do not open it, because it is not what this course demonstrates.

Sycophancy, the measured version. Sharma et al., ICLR 2024 (Anthropic), across five state-of-the-art assistants. When a user pushes back on a correct answer, models change their initial answer between 32% and 86% of the time depending on the model; a user who suggests a wrong answer drops accuracy by up to 27 points. It is a mechanism, not a motive: fine-tuning uses human judgements, and people prefer answers that confirm what they already think. It is the bridge to the third card of the user slide: the assumption put into the question comes back confirmed.

The "stochastic parrot" (Bender et al. 2021) belongs here if someone raises it: open debate, not verdict. Survey on hallucination: Ji et al. (2023).

user What happens to the person using it

Skill erosion

What is delegated gets practised less. Those who trust the tool check less; those who trust themselves check more.

The fluency trap

Polished text moves confidence, not accuracy — and moves it most outside one's own field.

The question enters the answer

An assumption planted in the question comes back as if established. Asking why something is true does not check that it is.

Make it concrete for a small business: the expert in the room spots a wrong attribution instantly. A junior with the same tool does not, and may never develop the eye if the tool always answers first. That is a staffing and training decision, not a technology one.

First card, and what kind of evidence it is. That we remember less of what we delegate is old and solid (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner 2011, Science, the Google effect). That expertise atrophies is not established, and should not be claimed. The closest data is Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al., CHI 2025 — 319 knowledge workers, 936 real episodes: more confidence in the tool goes with less critical thinking, more self-confidence with more of it. It is an association in a self-reported survey, and should be presented as one. Risko & Gilbert's review (2016) describes when people offload, not that the capacity decays.

Second card. Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh, CCS 2023 — controlled user experiment. Participants with the assistant wrote significantly less secure code and were more convinced they had written it securely; and those who trusted it less, and worked on how they phrased the prompt, produced fewer vulnerabilities. Code domain, 2022 model: say so. The second half of that result is the thesis of part C of the demo, minutes away.

Third card. It closes module 3's thread, "holidays" against "annual leave". There the words moved retrieval; here the assumption moves generation, because the prompt is the text the model continues. The example is said aloud, matched to the sector in the room: "why is this coin rarer?" · "why is this plant underperforming?" · "why did this test fail?" — none of the three checks that the premise is true.

The operating rule to dictate: ask "what is missing from this attribution?", not "confirm that this attribution is right".

data When text becomes an instruction

Prompt injection

An untrusted document carries a sentence the model executes instead of summarising.

Why it matters now

With the tools from Module 4 the model does not only answer: it opens files, writes, sends. Untrusted text stops being data and starts driving an action.

The line not to lose: while the model only writes text, a hidden instruction produces a wrong answer; once it has tools, it produces an action. Module 4's connectors widened the attack surface an hour earlier, in this same room.

Injection has no clean solution. It is not a bug closed by a patch: it follows from instructions and data reaching the model through the same channel, as text. So the design principle is least privilege — the tool sees only the folder it needs, and destructive actions live in the "never" tier of the table already seen in module 4.

Minutes later, part B of the demo runs the identical request of m4-p7 against the poisoned folder: the only changed variable is the document. Then the PDF opens, Ctrl+A, and the grey text is revealed. Do not pre-empt it: it has to derail in front of them.

Sources. Greshake et al. (2023), Indirect Prompt Injection; OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications.

The human in the loop

Why oversight slackens

The system is usually right, and attention moves elsewhere. It holds for experts as much as for novices, and it is not fixed by telling people to be careful.

What stopped it

Five minutes ago it was not an automated check: it was someone who knew the piece and could say no.

The same tool, the other way round

Have it argue the opposite, ask it what is missing, be interrogated by it. Those who interrogate it rather than delegate to it get better results.

The test

Oversight is real only if someone can understand, challenge and overturn. Missing any of the three, it is a rubber stamp.

The first card is the point worth defending, and it is measured: Parasuraman & Manzey, Human Factors 52(3) 381–410 (2010), a review integrating the empirical work on automation complacency. Two verbatim conclusions: complacency "is found in both inexperienced and expert participants and is not overcome by simple practice", and automation bias "is not prevented by training or by instructions". Put plainly in the room: experience does not protect you, and telling people to be careful is not a control. The remedy is not good intentions, it is design — the guardrail tiers from module 4.

The classic, to be offered as an argument and not as a measurement: Bainbridge, Ironies of Automation, Automatica 19(6) 775–779 (1983). The more reliable the automation, the less practised the operator stays at taking over precisely when they must. And vigilance cannot be intermittent: success on one task does not license trust on the next, because the boundary between them is declared nowhere.

The third card is the good news, and it should not be inflated. What is measured is that those who trusted less and worked on their phrasing got better results (Perry et al., CCS 2023), and that with AI the cognitive work does not vanish but shifts towards verification and oversight (Lee et al., CHI 2025). That this builds expertise over time is not established and should not be promised. What is promised is the posture — asking "what is missing" and "argue the opposite" instead of "confirm this" — the same operating rule dictated on the user slide.

The point lands best with their own client in mind: responsibility towards whoever receives the signed document is not delegated to a model, neither legally nor reputationally. The expert has just overturned it live: point at that moment, do not re-explain it.

The question

“One concrete thing you will do in the next 30 days applying what you learned today.”

Underneath it is probability. In a person's hands it is a tool. The hard part is judgement.

The chain is the last thing they read: read it aloud, one link at a time, without commentary. Link 4 says "then verify" and not "then let it act" on purpose — it is the conclusion module 4 actually ends on. Link 5 is the previous slide's test, word for word.

Attribution matters: Karpathy says explicitly that the line is a tweet he is quoting, not a formula of his own. Practising source hygiene on the final slide is worth ten recommendations.

Then the commitment question, which is why it is written on screen: the wording is identical to Q6.1 of the exit questionnaire, and it is the field the three-month follow-up rests on. It gets written, not said. A commitment spoken in the room leaves no trace; a written one does, and in three months it can be asked about.
