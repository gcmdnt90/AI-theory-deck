Module 2

Inside the model

What it is, how it is made, and what “reasoning” means.

The single most useful conceptual correction in the course. Almost every "can AI do X?" question is really a question about which side of this boundary X sits on.

Three levels, one machine

macro Judgement

The person who accepts or rejects the output.

→ “who answers if it is wrong?”

meso The system

Memory, tools, skills, connectors. This is where the work happens.

→ “why doesn't it know our price list?”

micro The base

Probabilities over the next piece of text. Always on.

→ “why does it miscount letters?”

Inside is the processor. Around is everything else — and around is where the work happens.

You cannot retrain a frontier model, and you do not need to. The scaffolding is built today, and it can be undone.

The test: does it change the model itself, or only what the model is given?

The deck's spine: the slide to defend if time runs short, and the only place the inside/around idea is stated. It used to be said twice, ten minutes apart, with two pictures and two vocabularies — that was the repetition the room felt. Said once here, everything later just refers back to it.

Cristianini's mesoscopic level: micro = neurons, meso = the abstract ideas the model built by itself, macro = behaviour read almost psychologically. The flank needs covering: this is a claim about the right level of description (Marr 1982), not that the model became a mind. "It's just statistics over words" is true at micro and useless at meso, which is where the listener's actual work happens.

The amber card is the commercial argument, and the good news for an SME: a frontier model is not something you retrain, and you do not need to. Everything that will make this useful in the business sits around it — and around is cheap, fast and reversible. Said explicitly, it reframes the day from "expensive AI project" to "configuration".

The takeaway is the test used for the rest of the day: did this change the model itself, or only what the model was given? The three arrows are the index of the remaining course: macro → module 5, meso → modules 3 and 4, micro → the module that just ended. This module is the left half; the rest of the day is the right half.

How a model is made

Yesterday — the only one we know in full

1. Reads textPredicts the next word across enormous amounts of writing.300 billion tokens
2. Learns to follow instructionsExamples teach it to carry out a task, not just continue text.~13,000 hand-written examples
3. Tuned by human feedbackPeople judge the answers; the model is pushed towards the preferred ones.~33,000 comparisons, ~40 people

Today

Llama 3: ~15 trillion tokens at step 1, fifty times InstructGPT — and a growing share of the examples in steps 2 and 3 is generated by other models, not written by people.

Knowledge comes from step 1. Assistant behaviour comes from steps 2 and 3.

The common belief in the room: "the model is one trained thing that knows facts". It gets corrected here — and it is worth self-correcting too, because the three numbers belong to one specific model: InstructGPT, 2022, the ancestor of ChatGPT, built on top of GPT-3. It is the only case in the family with a complete public account of all three steps. For other models they change by orders of magnitude: the shape of the pipeline holds, the figures do not.

The "Today" strip is the honest line of the module: it says that what is being learned has an expiry date. If time is short it can stay on screen without comment.

Two hooks worth keeping in a pocket. Step 3 pushes the model towards what the raters liked: that is where sycophancy comes from, not from any wish to please — and module 5 picks it up. The synthetic data of step 2 is the same thing behind the invented sheets used in today's demos.

Sources. Brown (2020) for GPT-3; Ouyang (2022) for the three numbers; Llama 3 (2024) for the "Today" strip; Christiano (2017) for human feedback.

"Reasoning": trained or prompted

inside Trained

Trained with reinforcement learning to deliberate before answering: self-checking and second thoughts emerge from the training, not from an instruction.

→ the “thinking” versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot; DeepSeek-R1 is the one with a public account

around Prompted

It is asked for in the prompt: “list the criteria first, then decide”.

→ free, on any model, including the free ones

The switch injects no prompt: it turns on a mode of the model — and today the model itself decides how much to think. Written instructions, task lists and loops do exist, but they are scaffolding: module 4.

The visible steps are produced text, not the minutes of how it got there.

What scrolls past on screen is a model-generated summary, not the internal trace (OpenAI).

Two of the three things are reasoning, the third is not, and the room confuses all three.

Prompted — asked for in the prompt, the model writes the steps as ordinary output, the model itself does not change.
Scaffolding — system prompt, instruction and personality files re-read on every request, task lists, loops, tools. It is real, but it is module 4, and it is not what makes a model a "reasoning" model.
Trained — reinforcement learning: the capability sits in the weights. DeepSeek-R1 shows it publicly, with pure RL and no human-written reasoning traces: self-verification, checking and strategy switching emerge from training. OpenAI says the same of o1: "our large-scale reinforcement learning algorithm teaches the model how to think productively using its chain of thought".

What the button does, if asked: it injects no prompt, it turns on a technical mode. On Claude it was a thinking-token budget (thinking + budget_tokens, up to 4.6); from 4.7 that parameter was removed and replaced by adaptive thinking with an effort level, and the model decides for itself whether and how much to think. On ChatGPT, picking the "thinking" version routes to a different model. Version numbers age in weeks: they live here, not on screen, and should be checked against the deck's edition date.

Why the takeaway. Turpin et al. (2023) slipped a biasing cue into the input — for instance the correct answer always in the same position. Models were steered by it, got the answer wrong, and never named the cue in the chain: they built a plausible justification for the wrong answer, up to −36% accuracy across 13 tasks. The cue is the measuring instrument, not advice for the audience, and does not go on screen.

This slide is the first deposit of the vigilance module 5 collects (the "fluency is not evidence of accuracy" item). Demo D2 lands it: the toggle is the trained kind, "list the criteria first" is the prompted kind.
