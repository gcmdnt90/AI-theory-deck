Module 5

Risks and judgement

What goes wrong and who stays responsible.

The last module, and the one that makes everything else defensible: the previous four showed what the machine can be made to do, this one says on what terms the result holds up in front of a client. Three risk owners - the model, the person using it, the data - and then who decides: that structure should survive even if every example is forgotten, and it is the one thing worth saying twice. This is not a module of warnings. Every risk named here already has its counter-move in the earlier modules: ground it in documents (M3), write the rules and verify (M4), keep destructive actions out of the tool's reach. Say so at the start, or the room stiffens and the closing demo reads as a punishment. Close on the commitment question: one thing each person will try in the next 30 days. Have it written down, not just spoken.

model What the model gets wrong

Hallucination

Fluent, confident, false. Structural, not a bug: it is reduced by anchoring it to documents and checking it. Not removed.

Bias

With no references of its own it returns the average of what has already been written: the words, patterns and structures the web is full of.

Agreeableness

Push back and it retracts — even when it was right. Finetuning rewards the answers people approve of.

The first two come from the engine of Module 1 — plausible text, not true text. The third comes from how it was finetuned: on human judgements.

The causal thread: in Module 1 they watched the engine sample from a distribution with no truth oracle attached. The first two cards come from there. Hallucination is also the correct answer to one of the ten test items - structural, reduced by grounding in documents and human verification, never removed: say it in those words. Bias, measured version: Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances 10(28), eadn5290 (2024) - an online experiment, peer-reviewed. With a single AI-generated idea, stories become more original for the person writing them (+8.1% novelty) but 10.7% more similar to each other. This is not a moral judgement on the training data: it is the average of what has already been written resurfacing whenever a reference of one's own is missing. It is why the Module 3 ladder changes the result, and why in the demo the disciplined prompt pulls a different structure out of the same photo. Where the model touches people - hiring, assessment, credit - bias also becomes a legal question (AI Act, high-risk systems): name it, do not open it, it is not what this course demonstrates. Agreeableness, measured version: Sharma et al., ICLR 2024 (Anthropic), across five state-of-the-art assistants. When a user challenges a correct answer, models change their initial answer between 32% and 86% of the time depending on the model; a user suggesting an incorrect answer cuts accuracy by up to 27 points. Mechanism, not motive: finetuning uses human judgements, and people prefer answers that confirm what they already think. It is the bridge to the third card on the user slide: an assumption planted in the question comes back confirmed. The "stochastic parrot" provocation (Bender et al. 2021) belongs here if someone raises it: a live debate, not a verdict. Hallucination survey: Ji et al. (2023).

user What happens to the person using it

Skill erosion

What is delegated gets practised less. Those who trust the tool check less; those who trust themselves check more.

The fluency trap

Polished text moves confidence, not accuracy — and moves it most outside one's own field.

The question enters the answer

An assumption planted in the question comes back as if established. Asking why something is true does not check that it is.

Make it concrete for a small business: the expert in the room spots a wrong attribution in an instant. A junior with the same tool does not, and may never develop the eye if the tool always answers first. That is a staffing and training decision, not a technology one. First card, and what kind of evidence it is: that what you offload you remember less is old and solid (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner 2011, Science, the Google effect). That the underlying competence decays is NOT established: do not say it. The closest evidence is Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al., CHI 2025 - 319 knowledge workers, 936 first-hand episodes: higher confidence in the tool goes with less critical thinking, higher self-confidence with more. It is an association in a self-reported survey, and must be said that way. The Risko & Gilbert (2016) review describes when people offload, not that the ability decays. Second card: Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh, CCS 2023 - a controlled user study. Participants with the AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code and were more likely to believe they had written secure code; and those who trusted it less, working on the wording of their prompts, produced fewer vulnerabilities. Domain is code and the model is from 2022: say so. The second half of that result is the thesis of part C of the demo, minutes away. Third card: closes the thread from Module 3, "ferie" versus "congedi annuali". There it was the words that moved retrieval; here it is the assumption that moves generation, because the prompt is the text the model continues. The example is spoken aloud, per the sector in the room: "why is this coin rarer?" - "why is this plant underperforming?" - "why did this test fail?" - none of the three checks that the premise holds. The operating rule to dictate: ask "what is missing from this attribution?", not "confirm that this attribution is right".

data When text becomes an instruction

Prompt injection

An untrusted document carries a sentence the model executes instead of summarising.

Why it matters now

With the tools from Module 4 the model does not only answer: it opens files, writes, sends. Untrusted text stops being data and starts driving an action.

The line not to lose: while the model only writes text, a hidden instruction produces a wrong answer; once it has tools, it produces an action. The connectors in Module 4 widened the attack surface an hour ago, in this same room. Injection has no clean fix - it is not a bug closed by a patch, it follows from instructions and data reaching the model through the same channel, as text. So you design for least privilege: the tool sees only the folder it needs, and destructive actions sit in the "never do" tier of the table they already saw in M4. In a few minutes part B of the demo runs the exact request of m4-p7 against the poisoned folder: the only changed variable is the document. Then open the PDF, Ctrl+A, and reveal the grey text. Do not pre-announce it: let it derail. Sources: Greshake et al. (2023), Indirect Prompt Injection; OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications.

The human in the loop

Why oversight slackens

The system is usually right, and attention moves elsewhere. It holds for experts as much as for novices, and it is not fixed by telling people to be careful.

What stopped it

Five minutes ago it was not an automated check: it was someone who knew the piece and could say no.

The same tool, the other way round

Have it argue the opposite, ask it what is missing, be interrogated by it. Those who interrogate it rather than delegate to it get better results.

The test

Oversight is real only if someone can understand, challenge and overturn. Missing any of the three, it is a rubber stamp.

The first card is the point worth defending, and it is measured: Parasuraman & Manzey, Human Factors 52(3) 381-410 (2010), a review integrating the empirical studies on automation complacency. Two verbatim conclusions: complacency "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice", and automation bias "cannot be prevented by training or instructions". Said in the room: experience does not protect, and telling people to pay attention is not a control. The remedy is not goodwill, it is design - the guardrail tiers they saw in M4. The classic, to be presented as an argument and not as a measure: Bainbridge, Ironies of Automation, Automatica 19(6) 775-779 (1983). The more reliable the automation, the less practised the operator remains at taking over exactly when they must. And vigilance cannot be intermittent: success on one task does not license trust on the next, because the boundary between the two is declared nowhere. Third card, the cheerful one, said without inflating it: what is measured is that those who trusted it less and worked on their phrasing got better results (Perry et al., CCS 2023), and that with AI the cognitive work does not vanish but shifts towards verification and oversight (Lee et al., CHI 2025). That this builds expertise over time is NOT established: do not promise it. Promise the posture - asking "what is missing" and "argue the opposite" instead of "confirm this" - which is the same operating rule dictated on the user slide. Say it with their own client in mind: responsibility towards whoever receives the signed document is not delegated to a model, neither legally nor reputationally. They have just watched the expert overturn it live: point at that moment, do not re-explain it.
